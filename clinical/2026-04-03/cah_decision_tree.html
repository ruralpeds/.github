CAH Overview

Congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH) due to 21-hydroxylase deficiency is the most common cause of primary adrenal insufficiency in children (incidence ~1:10,000–1:15,000). Classical forms (salt-wasting and simple virilizing) require lifelong glucocorticoid replacement. Patients cannot mount an adequate cortisol stress response and are at risk for life-threatening adrenal crisis during acute illness.

Why Sick Day Dosing Matters

The NIH longitudinal study of 156 CAH patients over 23 years found GI and upper respiratory infections were the two most common precipitants of adrenal crisis. During childhood, illness episodes and stress dosing were more frequent, but ER visits and hospitalizations were more common in adulthood — highlighting the importance of ongoing education. Adrenal crisis with probable hypoglycemia occurred in 11 pediatric patients aged 1–11 years.

Stress Dosing Principles

The goal of stress dosing is to mimic the physiological cortisol response to illness (~50–75 mg/m²/day), which is 3–5× the normal daily output of ~8–10 mg/m²/day. Hydrocortisone is preferred due to its mineralocorticoid activity and short half-life allowing dose titration.

Emergency IM Hydrocortisone by Age

  • Infants <1 year: 25 mg IM
  • Children 1–5 years: 50 mg IM
  • Children 6–12 years: 50–100 mg IM
  • Adolescents >12 years: 100 mg IM

Rural Considerations

In rural EDs and Critical Access Hospitals, CAH presentations may be rare. Ensure Solu-Cortef (hydrocortisone sodium succinate) is stocked in the Pyxis/ED. Endocrinology consultation by telemedicine should be initiated early. Transport teams should be notified if the patient meets adrenal crisis criteria. Families should carry emergency injectable hydrocortisone and a steroid emergency card at all times.
