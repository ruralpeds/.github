Key Evidence Summary

AES Guideline (Glauser et al., 2016): First-line benzodiazepines are Level A evidence. IM midazolam superior to IV lorazepam when no IV access (RAMPART trial). No significant difference between IV lorazepam and IV diazepam.

ESETT Trial (Kapur et al., NEJM 2019)

Landmark RCT: levetiracetam (60 mg/kg), fosphenytoin (20 mg/kg PE), and valproate (40 mg/kg) are equally effective as second-line therapy (~47-52% success in children). Any can be first-choice second-line agent.

Time-Dependent Treatment Response

  • 0-5 min: ~80% respond to first BZD
  • 5-10 min: ~60% respond to second BZD
  • 10-30 min (established SE): ~50% respond to 2nd-line
  • >30 min (refractory SE): <30% respond to 3rd-line
  • >24 hr (super-refractory): High morbidity/mortality

ILAE Neonatal Seizure Guidelines (2023)

Phenobarbital is first-line for neonatal seizures regardless of etiology (unless channelopathy suspected). Second-line options: phenytoin, levetiracetam, midazolam, or lidocaine. Discontinue ASMs before discharge for acute provoked seizures.

Seizure Action Plans

Boston Children's Hospital study (Stredny et al., 2025): Personalized seizure action plans reduced median time to first ASM (5 vs 16 min), shortened seizure duration (9 vs 22 min), and reduced intubation rate (14% vs 53%).

Rescue Medications (FDA-Approved)

  • Diastat (diazepam rectal gel) — Ages ≥2; 0.2-0.5 mg/kg
  • Nayzilam (midazolam nasal spray) — Ages ≥12; 5 mg/spray
  • Valtoco (diazepam nasal spray) — Ages ≥6; weight-based

Sick Day Principles

  • Fever, dehydration, illness lower seizure threshold
  • Ensure adequate hydration and antipyretics
  • Maintain all scheduled ASMs — DO NOT skip doses
  • Vomiting within 30 min of dose → repeat dose
  • Consider rectal/alternative route if persistent vomiting
  • Check for drug interactions with antibiotics/antivirals
  • Lower threshold to use rescue meds during illness
