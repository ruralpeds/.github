# Pediatric Intravenous Fluid Management: A Comprehensive Clinical Decision Reference

**Isotonic crystalloids are now the standard of care for maintenance IV fluids in children aged 28 days to 18 years, replacing decades of hypotonic fluid practice that caused preventable hyponatremic deaths.** The 2018 AAP Clinical Practice Guideline (PMID: 30478247), grounded in 17 RCTs involving 2,455 patients, established this paradigm shift with Grade A evidence. This reference synthesizes the best available evidence across 12 domains of pediatric fluid management — from routine maintenance to complex resuscitation scenarios — providing the clinical logic, formulas, protocols, and decision points needed to build both an educational resource and an interactive clinical decision tree for a Level II+ nursery and pediatric emergency CDS project.

---

## 1. The AAP 2018 guideline ended 60 years of hypotonic fluid tradition

### Key Action Statement and evidence basis

The AAP guideline (Feld LG et al., Pediatrics 2018;142(6):e20183083; PMID: 30478247; DOI: 10.1542/peds.2018-3083) contains a single formal Key Action Statement (KAS 1A): **children 28 days to 18 years requiring maintenance IVF should receive isotonic solutions with appropriate KCl and dextrose.** Evidence quality is **Grade A** with a **strong recommendation**, based on 16 of 17 RCTs demonstrating isotonic fluids' superiority in preventing hyponatremia.

The numbers-needed-to-treat tell the story: **7.5 patients** treated with isotonic (vs. hypotonic) fluids to prevent one case of hyponatremia (Na <135 mEq/L), and **27.8** to prevent moderate hyponatremia (Na <130 mEq/L). Isotonic solutions reduced hyponatremia incidence from >20% to <5%. Acceptable isotonic fluids include **0.9% NaCl** (154 mEq/L Na, 308 mOsm/L), **Plasma-Lyte** (140 mEq/L Na, 294 mOsm/L), and **D5 Lactated Ringer's** (130 mEq/L Na, 273 mOsm/L) — though LR was not directly tested in the reviewed trials.

Regarding monitoring: the guideline recommends serial serum sodium measurements beginning 6–12 hours after IVF initiation, even with isotonic fluids (hyponatremia still occurred in 0–23% of isotonic fluid recipients). If neurologic symptoms develop (nausea, headache, confusion, lethargy), electrolytes should be measured immediately. Dextrose (2.5–5%) should be included in maintenance fluids to prevent catabolism and ketosis.

### Why hypotonic fluids caused harm: the Holliday-Segar legacy

The Holliday-Segar formula (Pediatrics 1957;19(5):823–832; PMID: 13431307) calculated water requirements from caloric expenditure: **1 mL water per 1 kcal metabolized.** Electrolyte concentrations were estimated from human milk composition at 3 mEq Na per 100 kcal, producing a solution equivalent to D5 0.2% NaCl — a fluid that is **78% electrolyte-free water.** These were theoretical calculations for healthy children, never validated in acutely ill populations.

The mechanism of harm is straightforward. Hospitalized children experience non-osmotic AVP/ADH stimulation from **pain, nausea, surgical stress, hypovolemia, pneumonia, and CNS disorders** — roughly 30% of hospitalized febrile children meet SIAD criteria. Elevated ADH impairs free-water excretion. When hypotonic fluids deliver large volumes of electrolyte-free water that kidneys cannot clear, dilutional hyponatremia follows. Children are especially vulnerable because of their **larger brain-to-skull ratio**, leaving less space to accommodate cerebral edema. Moritz and Ayus documented over **50 cases of neurologic morbidity including 26 deaths** in children receiving hypotonic parenteral fluids (PMID: 12563043). The UK National Patient Safety Agency reported 4 deaths and 1 near-miss. Both ISMP Canada and the US issued safety alerts.

Key meta-analyses confirming the shift: **McNab et al. (Cochrane 2014; PMID: 25519949)** found isotonic fluids halved hyponatremia risk (RR 0.48); **Foster et al. (PMID: 24582105)** demonstrated hypotonic fluids carried RR 2.37 for hyponatremia and 6.1 for moderate hyponatremia; **Wang et al. (PMID: 24379232)** showed RR 5.29 for severe hyponatremia with hypotonic fluids. The largest RCT, **McNab's PIMS trial (Lancet 2015; PMID: 25472864)**, randomized 641 children and confirmed isotonic superiority without significant hypernatremia risk.

### Excluded populations requiring specialized protocols

The guideline explicitly excludes populations with complex sodium-water physiology:

- **Neonates <28 days**: Immature renal concentrating ability; isotonic fluids carry a **3.74× increased hypernatremia risk** in neonates (Amer et al. 2024 meta-analysis; PMID: 37365423)
- **Cardiac disease**: Impaired sodium/water excretion; volume overload risk at standard rates
- **Hepatic disease**: Hyperaldosteronism, impaired free-water excretion
- **Renal disease**: Variable sodium handling based on specific pathology
- **Neurosurgical patients**: High risk for SIADH, cerebral salt wasting, or diabetes insipidus
- **DKA, burns, severe diarrhea**: Each requires disease-specific fluid protocols detailed below

## 2. Holliday-Segar calculations remain the foundation for maintenance rates

### The 4-2-1 rule and daily calculation

The formula derives maintenance water needs from estimated caloric expenditure at rest:

**Hourly rates (4-2-1 rule):**
| Weight | Rate |
|--------|------|
| First 10 kg | 4 mL/kg/hr |
| 11–20 kg | 2 mL/kg/hr |
| >20 kg | 1 mL/kg/hr |

**Daily calculation:**
| Weight | Rate |
|--------|------|
| First 10 kg | 100 mL/kg/day |
| 11–20 kg | 50 mL/kg/day |
| >20 kg | 20 mL/kg/day |

A 25 kg child requires: 1000 + 500 + (5 × 20) = **1,600 mL/day** or approximately 67 mL/hr. The typical **maximum daily cap is ~2,400 mL/day** for large adolescents. **Ideal body weight should be used for obese patients** because adipose tissue has ~10% water content versus ~73% for lean tissue, and the original formula was derived from non-obese caloric expenditure.

### When to modify maintenance rates

**Restrict to 2/3 maintenance** for: SIADH, postoperative patients (elevated ADH persists ≥3 days), increased ICP, heart failure, and oliguric renal failure. **Restrict to 1/2 maintenance** for severe SIADH refractory to 2/3 restriction or anuria (insensible losses only ≈ 400 mL/m²/day).

**Increase above maintenance** for: fever (add **12% per °C above 37°C**), high-output renal failure, voluminous diarrhea, diabetes insipidus, and significant third-space losses.

## 3. Fluid resuscitation in septic shock: evidence now favors measured aggression

### Surviving Sepsis Campaign 2020 pediatric recommendations

The SSC pediatric guidelines (Weiss et al., Pediatr Crit Care Med 2020; PMID: 32032273; DOI: 10.1097/PCC.0000000000002198) differentiate management by resource setting. In **resource-rich settings**: administer **10–20 mL/kg isotonic crystalloid over 5–20 minutes**, reassess after each bolus, and repeat up to **40–60 mL/kg in the first hour.** If shock persists or fluid overload signs develop, initiate **epinephrine** (cold shock) or **norepinephrine** (warm shock). The guidelines **suggest balanced crystalloids over 0.9% saline** (weak recommendation, very low quality evidence).

In **resource-limited settings** without ICU availability, the SSC recommends **against routine bolus fluid** in children without hypotension — a direct response to the FEAST trial.

### The FEAST trial changed resource-limited practice

Maitland et al. (NEJM 2011; PMID: 21615299; DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa1101549) randomized 3,141 febrile children across East Africa. The results were striking: **48-hour mortality was 10.6% with albumin bolus, 10.5% with saline bolus, versus 7.3% with no bolus** (p=0.003). This 3.3% absolute risk increase — a **45% relative increase in mortality** — held across all subgroups including malaria, sepsis, and severe acidosis. The finding is context-specific: 57% had malaria, no ICU/ventilator support existed, and the disease profile differs from high-income settings. Nevertheless, it fundamentally reshaped WHO guidance for resource-limited settings.

### Balanced versus normal saline: the crystalloid debate

The **SMART trial** (Semler et al., NEJM 2018; PMID: 29485925) demonstrated that balanced crystalloids reduced the composite MAKE30 endpoint (death, new RRT, persistent renal dysfunction) versus saline in 15,802 critically ill adults: **14.3% vs 15.4%** (OR 0.90, p=0.04), with the benefit most pronounced in sepsis. Direct pediatric RCT evidence remains limited, but the physiologic rationale — avoiding hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis, renal vasoconstriction, and reduced GFR from high-chloride loads — supports the SSC's preference for balanced crystalloids.

### Fluid-responsive versus fluid-refractory shock

**Cold shock** (low cardiac output, high SVR) presents with cool mottled extremities, prolonged capillary refill, and weak pulses — **more common in children (~58–80%)** — and responds to epinephrine. **Warm shock** (high cardiac output, low SVR) presents with warm flushed skin, bounding pulses, and flash capillary refill — less common (~20%) — and responds to norepinephrine (Ceneviva et al., Pediatrics 1998; PMID: 9685464).

### Point-of-care ultrasound guidance

IVC collapsibility index >50% in spontaneously breathing patients suggests fluid responsiveness (sensitivity 71%, specificity 81% in adult data), but pediatric-specific validation is limited. IVC diameter varies significantly with age, and no universal pediatric cutoffs exist. **Lung ultrasound** detecting B-lines correlates with extravascular lung water and may be **superior to chest X-ray** for detecting fluid overload. POCUS should be used as part of a comprehensive assessment rather than as a standalone decision tool. Fluid overload signs mandating bolus cessation include: **hepatomegaly, rales, periorbital edema, weight gain >10%, gallop rhythm, and worsening oxygenation.**

## 4. Dehydration assessment requires structured clinical evaluation

### Severity classification and clinical signs

The AAP classifies dehydration in older children as **mild (3–5%)**, **moderate (6–9%)**, and **severe (≥10%)**, with higher thresholds for infants whose total body water is 70–80% of mass. The fundamental conversion: **% dehydration × 10 = fluid deficit in mL/kg** (e.g., 5% = 50 mL/kg deficit).

The Steiner et al. systematic review (JAMA 2004; PMID: 15187057) identified the best individual signs for ≥5% dehydration: **prolonged capillary refill** (LR+ 4.1), **abnormal skin turgor** (LR+ 2.5), and **abnormal respiratory pattern** (LR+ 2.0). No single sign is diagnostic; combination scoring performs better.

### Gorelick dehydration scales

The **4-item scale** (Gorelick et al., Pediatrics 1997; PMID: 9113963) uses capillary refill >2 seconds, absent tears, dry mucous membranes, and ill general appearance — ≥2 of 4 signs predicts ≥5% dehydration. The **10-item scale** adds sunken eyes, abnormal respirations, weak pulse, decreased skin elasticity, tachycardia, and decreased urine output — ≥3 of 10 predicts ≥5%, ≥7 predicts ≥10%. Sensitivity 87%, specificity 82% for the 10-item scale.

### Oral rehydration therapy: still underutilized in high-income settings

The **WHO reduced-osmolarity ORS** (245 mOsm/L: Na 75, K 20, Cl 65, citrate 10, glucose 75 mmol/L) reduced the need for unscheduled IV therapy by 33% compared to the original 311 mOsm/L formula. For mild dehydration: **50 mL/kg over 3–4 hours**; for moderate: **75–100 mL/kg over 4 hours**. Ondansetron dramatically improves ORT success — Freedman et al. (NEJM 2006; PMID: 16625009) showed a single oral dose reduced IV rehydration needs from 31% to 14%.

### IV rehydration and sodium-specific protocols

Standard IV rehydration follows three phases: **resuscitation** (20 mL/kg NS bolus if hemodynamically unstable), **deficit replacement** (½ deficit over first 8 hours, ½ over next 16 hours plus maintenance), and **ongoing losses** replacement mL-for-mL. The WHO Plan C for severe dehydration delivers 100 mL/kg LR: 30 mL/kg in the first 30–60 minutes, then 70 mL/kg over 2.5–5 hours depending on age.

**Hypernatremic dehydration (Na >150)** demands slow correction at **≤0.5 mEq/L/hr or ≤10–12 mEq/L per 24 hours**, corrected over 48–72 hours. Brain cells generate idiogenic osmoles (myo-inositol, taurine, glutamine) that take up to 36 days to fully dissipate. Rapid correction shifts water into brain cells, causing cerebral edema. Sodium should be checked every 4–6 hours during correction.

**Hyponatremic dehydration (Na <130)** requires correction at **≤8–10 mEq/L in the first 24 hours** to prevent osmotic demyelination syndrome (ODS). For acute symptomatic hyponatremia with seizures, **3% NaCl at 3–5 mL/kg over 10–20 minutes** raises sodium ~4–6 mEq/L acutely. The sodium deficit formula: **Na deficit (mEq) = 0.6 × weight (kg) × (desired Na − current Na).**

## 5. Perioperative fluid management has shifted toward restrictive, isotonic practice

### NPO deficit and intraoperative fluids

The traditional Furman approach calculates NPO deficit as maintenance rate × hours NPO, replaced as **½ in the first hour, ¼ in the second, ¼ in the third.** Modern practice with liberalized fasting guidelines (clear fluids up to 1–2 hours pre-op per ESA 2022; DOI: 10.1097/EJA.0000000000001599) has reduced NPO deficits substantially. Many anesthesiologists now administer **20–40 mL/kg isotonic fluid** during surgery and PACU without formal deficit calculation.

**Intraoperative maintenance** runs at 2/3 to full Holliday-Segar rates with isotonic crystalloid (LR preferred). Third-space replacement rates by surgery type — minor 2–4 mL/kg/hr, moderate 4–6 mL/kg/hr, major 6–10 mL/kg/hr — are used, though the third-space concept has been challenged in recent literature. Blood loss is replaced at **3:1 crystalloid-to-blood** or **1:1 colloid/PRBC-to-blood** ratios, with estimated blood volumes ranging from 90–100 mL/kg (premature) to 65–70 mL/kg (adolescent). The general transfusion trigger is **Hgb 7–8 g/dL** (TRIPICU study; Lacroix et al., NEJM 2007; PMID: 17442904).

**Pediatric ERAS protocols** emphasize shortened fasting, goal-directed fluid therapy, early oral intake, and minimizing IV fluid duration (Rove et al., Paediatr Anaesth 2018; PMID: 29752858).

## 6. DKA fluid management: the PECARN trial debunked the cerebral edema myth

### Initial resuscitation and the two-bag system

Current ISPAD 2022 guidelines (Glaser et al., Pediatr Diabetes 2022; PMID: 36250645; DOI: 10.1111/pedi.13406) recommend **10–20 mL/kg 0.9% NaCl over 20–30 minutes** for volume restoration, with additional boluses only if hemodynamically compromised. After stabilization, fluids run at **1.5× maintenance** (some protocols up to 2×), with the deficit evenly distributed over 24–48 hours. Unlike standard dehydration, replacement is **not front-loaded**.

The **two-bag system** (Grimberg et al., J Pediatr 1999; PMID: 10064682) uses Bag 1 (saline without dextrose) and Bag 2 (same saline with 10% dextrose), connected via Y-connector at a constant total rate. Clinicians titrate proportions to maintain glucose **150–250 mg/dL** while continuing insulin to clear ketones. Response time drops from ~42 minutes (traditional bag changes) to ~1 minute, with meta-analyses showing **reduced hypoglycemia (RR 0.50)** and shorter DKA resolution time.

DKA severity is classified as mild (pH 7.2–7.3, bicarb 10–15), moderate (pH 7.1–7.2, bicarb 5–10), and severe (pH <7.1, bicarb <5). When glucose drops to **250–300 mg/dL**, add dextrose to fluids — **never stop insulin**, which is needed to clear ketones.

### The PECARN FLUID trial: paradigm shift

Kuppermann et al. (NEJM 2018; PMID: 29897851; DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa1716816) randomized **1,255 children** in a 2×2 factorial design comparing rapid vs. slow rehydration and 0.9% vs. 0.45% saline. The results were definitive: **no significant difference** in GCS decline (RR 0.76, p=0.34 for rate; RR 0.80, p=0.43 for sodium content) or clinically apparent brain injury (0.9% overall). This challenged 20+ years of dogma that aggressive fluid resuscitation caused cerebral edema. Current understanding attributes cerebral injury to **neuroinflammation and ischemia-reperfusion** at presentation rather than osmotic fluid shifts from treatment.

### Potassium replacement and cerebral edema vigilance

Total body potassium is depleted (~5 mEq/kg) even when serum K⁺ appears normal or elevated at presentation. Protocol: if K⁺ >5.5 mEq/L, withhold potassium; if 3.5–5.5 mEq/L, add **20–40 mEq/L KCl or K-phosphate**; if <3.5 mEq/L, **hold insulin** and aggressively replace. Maximum peripheral concentration is 40 mEq/L; maximum infusion rate 0.5 mEq/kg/hr.

Cerebral edema occurs in **0.5–1% of pediatric DKA episodes** with **21–25% mortality** among those affected. Risk factors include younger age, new-onset diabetes, severe acidosis, elevated BUN, and failure of corrected sodium to rise during treatment. The Muir criteria (1 diagnostic or 2 major or 1 major + 2 minor criteria) achieve **92% sensitivity and 96% specificity**. Treatment: **mannitol 0.5–1 g/kg** over 15–20 minutes or **3% NaCl 2.5–5 mL/kg** over 10–15 minutes; elevate the head of bed; reduce fluid rate; **avoid aggressive hyperventilation** (Glaser et al., NEJM 2001; PMID: 11172153).

## 7. SIADH versus cerebral salt wasting: opposite diagnoses demand opposite treatment

### The critical clinical distinction

Both SIADH and cerebral salt wasting (CSW) present with hyponatremia, elevated urine sodium (>40 mEq/L), and low serum osmolality — but their treatments are diametrically opposed. **SIADH requires fluid restriction** (1/2 to 2/3 maintenance) while **CSW demands volume expansion** with NS at 1.5–2× maintenance or 3% NaCl. Treating CSW with restriction worsens hypovolemia and threatens cerebral perfusion.

| Parameter | SIADH | CSW |
|-----------|-------|-----|
| Volume status | Euvolemic/hypervolemic | **Hypovolemic** |
| Body weight | Stable/increased | Decreased |
| Urine output | Decreased/normal | **Polyuria** |
| CVP | Normal/high | Low |
| BUN | Low (dilutional) | **Elevated** |
| 24h urine Na excretion | ~51 mmol/24h | **~394 mmol/24h** |
| Fluid restriction response | Improves | **Worsens** |
| FEurate after Na correction | Normalizes (4–11%) | **Remains elevated (>11%)** |

The **fractional excretion of uric acid (FEurate)** after sodium correction is the most reliable differentiator: it normalizes in SIADH but remains elevated in CSW (Bardanzellu et al., Pediatr Nephrol 2022; PMID: 34468821). When uncertain, **treat as CSW** — volume expansion is safer than restriction in neurosurgical patients.

### Hypertonic saline for symptomatic hyponatremia

3% NaCl (513 mEq/L) given at **3–5 mL/kg over 10–20 minutes** raises serum sodium ~4–6 mEq/L and reliably terminates hyponatremic seizures. Sarnaik et al. (PMID: 2055051) demonstrated that 4–6 mL/kg boluses stopped seizures in all children, while conventional anticonvulsants alone failed in 13/28 cases. Brenkert et al. (PMID: 23283268) confirmed peripheral IV administration is safe for emergent boluses — 87% of pediatric ED doses were given peripherally. Total correction must not exceed **8–10 mEq/L per 24 hours** for chronic hyponatremia to avoid osmotic demyelination syndrome.

## 8. Burns, cardiac surgery, and renal disease each demand specialized fluid strategies

### Burns: Parkland formula with pediatric modifications

The Parkland formula delivers **4 mL/kg × %TBSA burned** over 24 hours (first half in the first 8 hours from time of burn, second half over the next 16 hours) using Lactated Ringer's. **Children <20 kg require additional maintenance fluids with D5LR** because limited glycogen stores predispose to hypoglycemia. The Galveston formula — **5,000 mL/m² TBSA burned + 2,000 mL/m² total BSA** — may be more physiologic for children given their higher BSA-to-mass ratio.

Urine output targets are **1–2 mL/kg/hr** (higher than the adult 0.5–1 mL/kg/hr). TBSA should be estimated using the **Lund-Browder chart**, which accounts for children's proportionally larger heads (19% in infants vs. 7% in adults). **Fluid creep** — over-resuscitation beyond calculated needs — causes abdominal compartment syndrome, pulmonary edema, and extremity compartment syndrome (Saffle, J Burn Care Res 2007; PMID: 17438489). Colloids (5% albumin) are typically introduced after 12–24 hours.

### Post-cardiac surgery: restrict, then mobilize

Standard practice is **50–75% of maintenance for the first 24–48 hours** post-bypass due to capillary leak syndrome — the systemic inflammatory response to cardiopulmonary bypass triggers complement activation (C3a, C5a), releasing IL-6, IL-8, and TNF-α. Approximately **50% of infants develop >10% fluid overload** after cardiac surgery, independently associated with increased mortality, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and AKI (Lex et al., PMID: 26890200). Furosemide (0.5–1 mg/kg bolus or 0.1–0.4 mg/kg/hr continuous infusion) targets negative fluid balance by **POD 2–3.** Prophylactic peritoneal dialysis post-CPB outperformed furosemide in preventing fluid overload in one RCT (Kwiatkowski et al., JAMA Pediatr 2017; PMID: 28241247).

### Renal disease: volume status guides every decision

In **nephrotic syndrome**, aggressive IV fluids worsen third-spacing. The albumin-furosemide protocol — **25% albumin 0.5–1 g/kg over 2–4 hours followed immediately by furosemide 1–2 mg/kg** — transiently raises oncotic pressure to mobilize interstitial fluid. Volume status assessment via FeNa is critical: <0.2% indicates volume contraction (needs albumin), while ≥0.2% indicates volume expansion (safe for diuretics alone) (Kapur et al., PMID: 19406963).

In **acute kidney injury**, fluid is restricted to **insensible losses (~400 mL/m²/day) plus measured urine output**. Potassium is withheld until serum K⁺ is known. Fluid overload >10% is associated with a **4.34× increase in mortality** in critically ill children (Alobaidi et al., JAMA Pediatr 2018; PMID: 29356810).

## 9. Electrolyte requirements and IV fluid composition reference

### Maintenance electrolyte needs

Daily requirements: **sodium 2–3 mEq/kg/day** (neonates may need 2–5 mEq/kg/day), **potassium 1–2 mEq/kg/day** (never as bolus; maximum peripheral concentration 40 mEq/L, maximum rate 0.5 mEq/kg/hr with cardiac monitoring). Standard maintenance dextrose is **D5 (5%)**, providing ~170 kcal/L — sufficient to prevent catabolism and ketosis but supplying only ~17% of daily caloric needs. **D10** is preferred for neonates and infants at hypoglycemia risk; glucose infusion rate (GIR) is calculated as (% dextrose × rate mL/hr) / (6 × weight kg), targeting 4–6 mg/kg/min in term neonates.

### IV fluid compositions

| Fluid | Na (mEq/L) | Cl (mEq/L) | K (mEq/L) | Ca (mEq/L) | Buffer | Glucose (g/L) | Osmolality (mOsm/L) |
|-------|-----------|-----------|----------|-----------|--------|--------------|---------------------|
| **0.9% NaCl** | 154 | 154 | 0 | 0 | None | 0 | 308 |
| **Lactated Ringer's** | 130 | 109 | 4 | 2.7 | Lactate 28 | 0 | 273 |
| **Plasma-Lyte A** | 140 | 98 | 5 | 0 | Acetate 27, Gluconate 23 | 0 | 294 |
| **D5W** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | None | 50 | 252 |
| **D5 0.9% NaCl** | 154 | 154 | 0 | 0 | None | 50 | 560 |
| **D5 0.45% NaCl** | 77 | 77 | 0 | 0 | None | 50 | 406 |
| **D5 0.2% NaCl** | 34 | 34 | 0 | 0 | None | 50 | ~321 |
| **D5LR** | 130 | 109 | 4 | 2.7 | Lactate 28 | 50 | 525 |
| **3% NaCl** | 513 | 513 | 0 | 0 | None | 0 | 1026 |
| **0.45% NaCl** | 77 | 77 | 0 | 0 | None | 0 | 154 |
| **D10W** | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 | None | 100 | 505 |

**Effective versus actual osmolality** is clinically critical: dextrose is rapidly metabolized intracellularly and becomes free water in vivo. D5W has an actual osmolality of 252 mOsm/L but an **effective osmolality of ~0** — it is physiologically equivalent to free water. D5NS (560 mOsm/L in the bag) functions as isotonic saline (~308 mOsm/L effective) after glucose metabolism.

## 10. Monitoring protocols and safety considerations

### Lab monitoring frequency

Baseline BMP (Na, K, Cl, CO₂, BUN, Cr, glucose) should be obtained before starting IVF. For standard maintenance: BMP at 24 hours, then at least every 48 hours. For abnormal electrolytes: every 4–8 hours. For DKA: every 1–2 hours initially. For hyponatremia correction with 3% NaCl: every 2–4 hours. Urine specific gravity and urine electrolytes (UNa, UOsm) are indicated for hyponatremia workup.

### Transition from IV to oral and access considerations

Transition to oral fluids when vomiting resolves, mental status is adequate, and reliable oral intake reaches >50% of maintenance needs. IV access flow rates by gauge: **24G ~20–25 mL/min, 22G ~30–40 mL/min, 20G ~60 mL/min, 18G ~100 mL/min.** Intraosseous access supports all resuscitation medications and crystalloids under pressure. 

High-risk fluids for extravasation injury include D10 and above, calcium salts, potassium >40 mEq/L, vasopressors, and TPN. Treatment includes immediate infusion cessation, elevation, and **hyaluronidase** (150 units/mL, 1–1.7 mL intradermally) for calcium, hypertonic dextrose, and TPN extravasation. **Phentolamine** (0.1–0.2 mg/kg diluted in NS) is specific for vasopressor extravasation. Key medication incompatibilities to hard-code into any CDS: calcium + bicarbonate (precipitates), calcium + phosphate (precipitates), calcium + ceftriaxone (contraindicated in neonates), phenytoin + dextrose-containing fluids, and amphotericin B + saline.

## Conclusion: building the decision tree from evidence

This body of evidence supports a clinical decision tree with several key branching points. The **first node** should determine whether the patient falls within the AAP guideline population (28 days–18 years, no excluded conditions) or requires a specialized pathway. For standard maintenance, the path is now clear: **isotonic crystalloid with 5% dextrose and 20 mEq/L KCl at Holliday-Segar rates**, adjusted for clinical context. The PECARN FLUID trial's finding that neither fluid rate nor sodium content affects neurological outcomes in DKA eliminates a major historical concern and simplifies that pathway. The SIADH-versus-CSW branch — where identical labs demand opposite treatments — is perhaps the highest-risk decision node and should prominently feature volume status assessment and FEurate trending.

Three overarching principles emerge: **(1)** isotonic fluids are safer than hypotonic in virtually all pediatric maintenance scenarios; **(2)** fluid overload >10% body weight consistently predicts worse outcomes across cardiac surgery, AKI, and sepsis; and **(3)** correction rate limits for dysnatremias (≤0.5 mEq/L/hr for hypernatremia, ≤8–10 mEq/L/24h for hyponatremia) are bright-line safety boundaries that the decision support system should enforce with hard stops. The shift from protocol-based to physiology-based, goal-directed fluid therapy — guided by clinical reassessment, POCUS, and serial labs — represents the frontier of pediatric fluid management.
