## Overview

The evolution from near-universal mechanical ventilation of premature neonates toward less invasive strategies has transformed outcomes in neonatal intensive care. This guide covers the physiological rationale, clinical indications, setup procedures, monitoring, troubleshooting, and weaning for all major non-invasive ventilation modalities used in the NICU and special care nursery.

### Historical Context

- 1971: Dr. George Gregory (UCSF) — first description of CPAP for neonates
- 1980s–1990s: Scandinavian experience demonstrating early CPAP with selective surfactant
- 2008: COIN trial; 2010: SUPPORT trial — confirmed early CPAP as safe alternative to routine intubation
- Present: NIV is first-line respiratory support in most NICUs worldwide

### Physiological Rationale

The fundamental principle underlying all forms of NIV is maintenance of functional residual capacity (FRC). Goals: provide minimum distending pressure to maintain FRC, optimize oxygenation, and reduce work of breathing — without over-distending the lungs.

## Modalities Covered

### 1. CPAP (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure)

**Indications**:
- Primary respiratory support at birth for infants <30 weeks with spontaneous effort
- Delivery room stabilization (alternative to routine intubation/prophylactic surfactant)
- Transitional respiratory distress in late preterm and early term infants
- TTN requiring supplemental oxygen
- Post-extubation support
- Apnea of prematurity (unresponsive to methylxanthines alone)
- Surfactant administration strategies (INSURE, LISA, MIST)
- Tracheomalacia/bronchomalacia, laryngomalacia, Pierre Robin sequence
- Post-operative respiratory support
- BPD requiring ongoing positive pressure

**Initial Settings by GA**:
| GA | Starting CPAP | Max before escalation |
|----|--------------|----------------------|
| 23–32 weeks | 5–6 cmH₂O | 8 cmH₂O |
| Late preterm | 4–6 cmH₂O | 8 cmH₂O |
| Term | 4–5 cmH₂O | 8 cmH₂O |
| Post-extubation ELBW | 7–9 cmH₂O (up to 11) | — |

**Escalation threshold**: FiO₂ >0.30–0.40 on CPAP ≥6 cmH₂O → surfactant administration (LISA or INSURE)

### 2. NIPPV (Nasal Intermittent Positive Pressure Ventilation)

**Settings**: PIP 14–25 cmH₂O, PEEP 5–6 cmH₂O, Rate 20–40 bpm, Ti 0.3–0.5 sec

**Evidence**: Cochrane meta-analysis — synchronized NIPPV superior to CPAP for preventing extubation failure (RR 0.21, 95% CI 0.10–0.45, NNT 3). Kirpalani NIPPV Trial (NEJM 2013, n=1,009) showed subgroup analysis favoring ventilator-generated synchronized NIPPV.

### 3. BiLevel / BiPAP

Alternates high pressure (8–12 cmH₂O) and low pressure (4–6 cmH₂O) at 10–30 cycles/min. Evidence insufficient to determine advantage over standard CPAP. Network meta-analysis shows both synchronized and non-synchronized NIPPV superior to BiPAP for reintubation prevention.

### 4. HFNC (High-Flow Nasal Cannula)

Flow rates 2–8 L/min for neonates. CPAP is superior to HFNC for preventing treatment failure (RR 1.83 favoring CPAP), so HFNC should be step-down from CPAP rather than replacement.

## Weaning Protocol

Stepwise sequence: Invasive ventilation → NIPPV → NCPAP → HFNC → Low-flow NC → Room air

**NIPPV weaning**: Rate by 2–4 bpm q6h when PIP ~12–15, PEEP 5–6, FiO₂ <0.30
**CPAP to HFNC**: Stable on 4–5 cmH₂O for 12–24 hours
**HFNC weaning**: 2–6 L/min → low-flow 0.25–1 LPM → room air

**Extubation failure rates**: 40–50% ELBW; 25–35% 27–28 weekers; <15% late preterm
